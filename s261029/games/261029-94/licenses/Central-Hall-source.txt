How Sound Travels
Sound travels as
vibrations through a medium
(material), such as air or water. These vibrations push the nearby particles back and forth.
The animations below show how the sound travels in two dimensions and three dimensions.
In a
transverse wave
, the
movement of particles is perpendicular to the direction the wave travels
. For example, if you shake one end of a rope up and down, the wave moves horizontally from left to right, but each part of the rope only moves vertically up and down. The particles themselves do not travel along the rope; instead, each particle simply vibrates around its own fixed position as the energy passes through it.
When it comes to music and audio, however, sound waves behave a bit differently. Sound travels as a
longitudinal wave
, meaning the
air particles vibrate back and forth in the same direction that the wave is travelling
. These vibrations travel through the air, naturally getting weaker and losing energy the further they go, until they finally reach our ears and we perceive them as sound.

There are three primary components of how sound travels through an environment:
Reflections
are like sound hitting a mirror. Just as a mirror reflects light, sound can reflect off of surfaces. Sound reflects better off of hard, smooth surfaces like tables and walls, and the angle the sound bounces off the surface is the same as the angle it hits the surface.
Diffusion
is like a broken mirror for sound. Just like the broken mirror might scatter light in lots of directions, diffusion scatters sound from a rough surface, like a stone wall, into different directions as it disperses its energy.
Absorption
is like a sponge for sound. Like water being soaked up into a sponge, the sound is absorbed. When sound hits a soft surface, like curtains or a blanket, absorption converts the sound energy into heat.
Room Acoustics and Room Impulse Response
Have you ever noticed as you enter a building, your ears can gather information about the space you are in from the way things sound? Your ears listen to the reflections, diffusion, and absorption of sounds that’s happening in the space. A combination of all three of these components occurs in almost every space that you enter.
A theatre where actors are performing a show will usually have a carpet on the floor. This is because it can absorb the harsher parts of sound and help create an environment that is suitable for listening to dialogue.  Alternatively, in a cave, the stone walls reflect sound, so we perceive the space as reverberant or 'echoey'.
Room acoustics
is a
study of understanding how sound behaves in different spaces
, and
Room Impulse Response (RIR)
is a tool we can use to do this.
Acoustic Impulse Response (IR)
can be considered as the ‘acoustic fingerprint’ of a space.
IR describes how a space responds to sound within it
.
Before we explore impulse response further, we need to think about how sound travels in space. There are three stages: direct sound, early reflections and reverberation.

Recording Impulse Response
An
impulse
is a
short but loud signal that is used to excite a room
. It allows us to listen to how the room responds to the impulse, and tell us about the acoustic characteristics of the space.
There are two main ways to record IRs: the
transient method
and the
exponential sine sweep method
.
1) The transient method: using natural physical acoustics, such as hand claps, balloon pops, or a starter pistol.

ESS can be played back over a loudspeaker in a room of interest, and recorded using a microphone. After we obtain the recording, an audio process called
convolution
can be used to achieve the room impulse response (RIR).
Apart from listening to resulting IRs, we can also do further IR analysis to understand the acoustic character of the space, such as
reverberation time
.
Reverberation Time
Historical Context
Using an organ pipe and stopwatch, Wallace Sabine (1868-1919) pioneered reverberation time measurement. Wallace used the organ pipe as his impulse, and the stopwatch the time how long the sound took to die away. This time is called the
reverberation time
, a concept that is still widely used as a character of room acoustics. He es
tablished the now-standard measurement of reverberation time known as
RT60
:
the time taken for a sound in a space to reach one millionth of its original intensity,
equivalent to a decrease of 60 dB
.
Decibels (dB) are a unit used to express the ratio between two sound levels or intensities.
The factors effecting reverberation time include both the
size of the space
and the
amount of sound absorbed at each reflection.
The ideal RT60 of a concert hall = 2 - 2.25 seconds.
The ideal RT60 of a lecture hall = less than 1 second.

Suggested at-home experiment
Now that you have learned how to record impulse responses, you could go out and clap your hands or pop some balloons in different spaces. Try and listen to the early reflections from the hard surfaces like concrete walls: what’s the reverberation time (RT) of each space? Why do some spaces have a longer RT than others?
Application of RIR: Auralisation
Auralisation is the virtualisation of acoustics. Just as a 3D render allows us to preview a building's design, auralisation allows us to preview how a specific space will sound before we ever step foot inside.
The primary purpose of auralisation is to test the suitability of a room for its intended use. For example, if we are designing a lecture hall, we need to know if the acoustics support clear, intelligible speech. By creating an auralisation, we can
'
pre-listen
'
to a lecture in that virtual space and determine if the room’s reflections will make the speaker easy to understand.
To achieve this, we use the Room Impulse Response as the acoustic blueprint, which contains all the data about how sound bounces off the walls, ceiling, and floor. For a precise simulation, we must use an anechoic recording as our sample sound. These recordings are captured in specialised environments like the anechoic chamber at the University of York (pictured below).
An anechoic chamber is designed with deep foam wedges that absorb nearly 100% of sound reflections.
By using an anechoic source, we ensure that the final auralisation is untainted. When you listen to the result, you are hearing only how the chosen virtual space responds to the audio, providing insight into the  room's acoustic performance.
Listen to the recording below of singing in an anechoic chamber. Notice how crisp the sound is: that’s because almost all of the sound reflections are being absorbed by the walls.
